Japan in November

For those of you who are traveling from afar to be with us, we hope you will be able to reserve some time to explore what Japan has to offer. We put this guide together just for that reason.

Japan has so much to see and do that it is impossible to do everything in one visit. Find what pulls you in, do a little digging, and build the trip that fits your timeline. These are our honest, opinionated notes—take what is useful and ignore the rest.

Why November works

November is among the driest, clearest, most comfortable months. Expect blue-sky days in the 50s and low 60s °F, mornings and evenings in the 40s, and layers plus a light jacket rather than a heavy coat.

You are arriving in peak koyo. The fall colors are more vivid than anything we have seen in the US, while cold, dry air brings some of the clearest Mt. Fuji views of the year.

Eat the season

Look for persimmons, mikan, roasted sweet potatoes (yaki-imo), chestnuts, new-harvest rice, and the start of crab and oyster season. Grab late Shine Muscat grapes if you see them; toward month’s end, watch for winter’s first premium strawberries. More on food below!

If you ask us

Our recommendation

Every trip will look a little different, but the shape is simple: anchor on Tokyo and the wedding, then give whatever time remains to Kyoto and Osaka.

The Osaka math

Osaka is a day trip, not a base

Osaka is an easy day trip from Kyoto, and that is how we would plan it. If the neon and the food pull you in for longer, sleep there one or two nights at most and give Kyoto the rest.

Before you go

A little planning pays off

Japan rewards advance planning more than almost anywhere we have traveled—mostly because the good stuff books up.

01Two to three months out

  • Passport, flights, hotels

    Check that your passport and visas are valid through the trip; book flights and hotels, including at least one night at a ryokan.

  • Check your medications

    Japan bans some common US medicines: pseudoephedrine decongestants (regular Sudafed) and ADHD stimulants like Adderall, prescription or not. Vyvanse and more than a month's supply of many prescriptions need an import certificate that takes weeks, so check yours early.

    Check Japan's medicine rules ↗

  • Dinner reservations

    Book ahead

    Start with the restaurants you care about. Windows typically open 30–60 days ahead; find each opening date and set a reminder.

  • Timed attractions

    Book ahead

    Book teamLab, Shibuya Sky, the Sagano Romantic Train, Rurikoin, and Universal Studios as their windows open.

02One month out

  • Shinkansen seats

    Book ahead

    Reservations generally open one month ahead and advance tickets are cheaper. Choose the Mt. Fuji side and reserve oversized-luggage seats if needed; wrestle with the Japanese booking site from your couch, not a station.

  • Visit Japan Web

    Enter immigration and customs information and get an arrival QR code; it saves real time at the airport.

    Set up Visit Japan Web ↗
03One to two weeks out

  • Data

    Set up an eSIM (we use Airalo) unless your phone plan includes international data.

  • Suica

    Use this tap-to-pay transit card for trains, convenience stores, Starbucks, groceries, and more. iPhone users can add Suica in Apple Wallet (Wallet → + → Transit Card → Suica); most US Android phones cannot run Mobile Suica, so pick up a physical Welcome Suica at the airport.

  • Useful apps

    Download Google Maps, Google Translate with the Japanese offline pack, and the GO taxi app.

  • Cards

    Bring a Visa or Mastercard with no foreign transaction fees. Plenty of card-taking places do not accept AMEX.

04What to pack

  • Walking shoes

    Your comfiest pair: 15,000–20,000 steps a day is completely normal.

  • Layers

    Pack for crisp days and chilly mornings and evenings; a light jacket beats a heavy coat.

  • Coin pouch

    Coins pile up and remain useful for vending machines, lockers, and all sorts of small purchases.

  • Water bottle

    Restaurant water pours tend to be tiny, so a reusable bottle earns its luggage space.

  • Plug check

    US two-prong Type A plugs work with Japan’s 100V outlets; three-prong plugs need a cheap adapter.

Cash

Carry yen even when cards work

Souvenir shops, snack stands, and street-food counters can still be cash-only. ¥10,000–20,000 per person per week is a good baseline; the exchange rate has been very favorable to the dollar for the last few years. If you run low, ATMs inside any 7-Eleven accept US cards.

The essentials

Know before you go

  • A little Japanese goes a long way

    Very few people speak English, and even those who do may prefer not to. Learn a handful of phrases and use Google Translate when needed. Most signs are in English and tourist areas are well prepared for foreigners.

  • Follow the rules

    Japan is very rule-oriented: people even wait at an empty red crosswalk. Be on time—not early or late—and be polite, like a guest in someone else’s home. Foreigners get a free pass on most things.

  • Expect a price dichotomy

    Most things feel remarkably cheap compared with the US, while taxis, fancy fruit, Kobe beef, and nice hotels can get fairly pricey. Overall, your money goes surprisingly far.

  • Shop tax-free; carry your passport

    Most stores return the tax at the register, but you need your actual physical passport. Bring it on days when you may do real shopping.

  • Almost no trash cans

    Carry your trash until you find one. Konbini—7-Eleven, Lawson, and FamilyMart—are reliable places to dispose of it. Somehow the country remains spotless.

  • Two restaurant quirks

    Water rarely appears unless you ask, and it comes in a tiny cup. If you take milk or cream in coffee, ask for extra; the default pour is comically small.

Getting around

Getting around

  • Fly into Haneda if you can

    Both Tokyo airports work, but Haneda sits about 30 minutes from the center while Narita is its own 60–90 minute leg by express train or limousine bus. Prefer Haneda when fares are close, and budget the extra time if you land at Narita.

  • Google Maps is your friend

    It is accurate for navigating Japan and tells you which numbered station exit to use—which matters more than you would think.

  • Giant stations need buffer time

    Major stations are extremely large and genuinely confusing, sometimes with 50 numbered exits and multiple separate train systems. A transfer can mean leaving one system and entering another without going outdoors. Leave time to get lost once or twice.

  • Book the big train trips at home

    Book ahead

    Buy city-to-city tickets such as Tokyo → Kyoto online before the trip; it is cheaper and avoids Japanese booking sites on vacation. Buy or tap Suica for local subways and short hops as you go.

  • Ride the shinkansen at least once

    Book ahead

    It is the way to make big hops such as Tokyo–Kyoto. Reserve ahead if the Mt. Fuji view matters to you; November’s clear-view odds are excellent.

  • Skip the JR Pass

    Old-school advice says get the Japan Rail Pass; after its 2023 price hike it almost never pays off on a Tokyo–Kyoto–Osaka trip. Point-to-point tickets are the cheaper, simpler move.

  • Kyoto ↔ Osaka, the scenic way

    Book ahead

    The plush Aoniyoshi sightseeing train runs among Kyoto, Nara, and Osaka and is worth taking for the experience. Book it in advance.

  • Taxis in moderation

    They are readily available but pricey, so default to walking and trains and use taxis strategically to save time and feet. GO works well. Drivers’ GPS can be charmingly old-school, making rides longer than Google estimates, and there is no Uber-style rideshare—every ride is an official taxi.

  • Trains stop around midnight

    The rail network sleeps from roughly midnight until 5am—there is no all-night subway. Out late in Shinjuku or Dotonbori, check your last train in Google Maps; missing it means a long, surcharged taxi ride home.

The luggage move

Send the suitcase. Carry one night.

Hotels can forward bags overnight to your next hotel for a reasonable fee. It is a game-changer because stations and trains are not built for large suitcases. Shinkansen oversized-luggage seats must be reserved specifically. Forward the big bag and keep a small overnight bag with you. For day trips and checkout days, station coin lockers (¥300–800; many take Suica) hold bags while you sightsee.

Only-in-Japan favorites

Experiences worth building around

  • Stay at a ryokan for a night

    A traditional inn is worth choosing over the familiar international brands for one night. Styles and prices vary widely; dinner and breakfast are generally included and often spectacular multi-course affairs. Many have onsen.

  • Go to an onsen

    This natural hot-spring bath is one of our favorite experiences in Japan. Our hotel’s onsen is currently under renovation, so we’re planning to visit another nearby onsen during the wedding weekend. Public baths are gender-separated, everyone is naked, and phones stay away. Private baths can be rented by the hour or built into nicer ryokan rooms. Find an outdoor rotenburo for steaming water in cool November air. For a public bath, watch an etiquette video beforehand—there are real rules and no phone inside to check them. If you have tattoos, check the bath’s policy first: many public baths still turn away visible ink. Small pieces hide under cover stickers—Don Quijote sells them—and a private bath sidesteps the question entirely.

    Wedding weekend onsen details ↗

  • Go to a wet head spa

    Japan has spas devoted entirely to the head and scalp. Dry and wet versions are both great, but choose wet if you must pick. The temperature-change wizardry is among the most relaxing things we have experienced; a good one costs a decent chunk and is 100% worth it.

  • Wander a giant Don Quijote

    Try a large Tokyo or Osaka branch: Amazon in real life, with nearly everything, usually cheap, and tax-free buying in-store. Even navigating it without buying anything is an experience.

  • Animal cafés

    Order a drink and spend time with the residents. We have enjoyed cat cafés; dog, hedgehog, mini pig, otter, and owl versions also exist if you go looking.

Eating & drinking

Your guide to picking restaurants

  • Yakiniku / wagyu
  • Yakitori
  • Tonkatsu
  • Kaiten sushi
  • Japanese fruit
  • Kakigori
  • Japanese whisky

  • Restaurants specialize

    Menus are not diverse in the US sense: you choose what you are eating when you choose the restaurant. Many are course-based. The quality bar runs high from vending-machine-ticket ramen counters to Michelin stars.

  • Reserve what matters most

    Book ahead

    Top places combine tiny seating counts with huge demand, confusing sites, and fierce competition; popular no-reservation spots mean a line. Lock in dinners for the food you care most about, then leave other meals for casual finds. We prioritize teppanyaki, yakitori, tonkatsu, and yakiniku because we love meat—make the list your own.

  • Try kaiten sushi

    Order on a tablet and watch your choices arrive by conveyor belt. It sounds gimmicky; the sushi is actually really good. Omakase is the fancy chef-decides-everything alternative, but can overwhelm anyone who does not want a large range of fish.

Photo credit: Chris 73 via Wikimedia Commons, licensed under CC BY-SA 3.0. Cropped and converted to WebP for this Guide.

  • Look for the Kobe stamp

    Kobe is a specific wagyu from a specific breed, not a synonym for all Japanese beef. It is pricey and the best steak either of us has had. Omi and Matsusaka are other premium types—we have tried all three and found them fairly indistinguishable—but Kobe is easiest on a tourist route. Teppanyaki is ideal. Look for the official stamp: a small golden cow statue proving legitimacy.

  • Take Japanese fruit seriously

    Fruit is sold in grades; the top ones are pricey and worth trying once. Find beautifully packed fruit in supermarkets, department-store basements, and stations. Premium strawberries arrive toward winter at roughly ¥1,000–1,500 for six (about $10). We can barely eat strawberries at home now. Also try Shine Muscat grapes, muskmelon, apples, and mikan.

  • Try kakigori

    Japanese shaved ice is way better than versions we have tried elsewhere. It comes in every flavor; green tea is the classic.

  • Explore Japanese whisky by the pour

    Bottles can oddly cost more in Japan than in the US, but bar and restaurant pours are much cheaper. Try Yamazaki, Hakushu, and Hibiki. Distillery tours use a lottery that requires applying months ahead.

City 01

Tokyo

Tokyo’s scale makes NYC feel small. A single neighborhood can fill half or all of a day, so do not check off everything—pick what sounds like you.

Things to see · in no particular order

  • Shibuya & Shibuya Crossing

    Explore the neighborhood and see the crossing at full chaos. The huge Tower Records supplies ’90s nostalgia.

  • Shibuya Sky

    Book ahead

    The open-air deck above the crossing reveals Tokyo’s immense scale. Buy early; popular slots sell out. Arrive about an hour before sunset—mid-afternoon in November—for daylight, sunset, and night.

  • Harajuku

    A great neighborhood for shopping and restaurants.

  • Shinjuku

    Night

    Go when the neon and nightlife are fully lit.

  • Senso-ji Temple (Asakusa)

    Arrive in late afternoon while the shopping street is open, then stay for the illuminated temple and pagoda.

  • teamLab Planets

    Book ahead

    The immersive art exhibit lives up to its Instagram fame and takes about two hours; tickets are required in advance. The newer teamLab Borderless also gets great reports, though we have not done it yet.

  • Tokyo Metropolitan Government Building

    Walk-in

    A free Shinjuku-area observation deck with no advance ticket. We think Shibuya Sky is much better, but the price is right.

  • Imperial Palace

    Skip the formal tours; walk the grounds and enormous park when the light is good.

  • Tokyo Station

    Enormous enough to be a destination, with lots of shopping inside and a facade beautifully lit at night.

  • Tsukiji Outer Market

    Graze through the stalls at lunch. It occupies the old fish-market site, so the seafood is famously fresh.

  • Ginza

    Optional

    Tokyo’s luxury shopping district; an easy time-saving skip if luxury shopping is not your thing.

  • Akihabara

    Optional

    A must for video-game or anime fans, and an easy skip for everyone else.

  • Tokyo Tower

    Do not bother going up. See it from nearby and visit the lovely Zojo-ji temple.

  • Tokyo Disney Resort

    Optional

    We have not visited, but it supposedly has some of the best Disney parks anywhere—especially DisneySea—if that is your thing.

Where to eat · the must-gos

Food quality is high almost everywhere, but a few high-end experiences truly stand out. These are those.

  • Yoroniku Ebisu

    Book ahead

    Yakiniku (Japanese BBQ), and perhaps our single favorite restaurant in Japan. Choose the course with the gyukatsu sando. Reservations are required and semi-hard to get.

  • Kaiten Sushi Ginza Onodera

    The conveyor-belt offshoot of a famous Michelin-starred omakase house. If you do one kaiten sushi in Japan, make it this one. Walk-in. Also in Kyoto.

  • Torisho Ishii Hina

City 02

Kyoto

The former capital is Japan’s historical heart: temples, shrines, and old streets. The supply is effectively endless, so research and choose what calls to you. The single biggest tip: reach major sights as early as possible. Crowds get intense.

The November move

Reserve early-morning and evening temple viewings

Kyoto is one of the world’s most famous foliage destinations, so peak koyo brings everyone and their mother. Many temples, parks, and shrines offer special reserved early-morning and evening viewings; evening sessions are often illuminated and avoid the general crowds. They require booking ahead. We would do this every single time: look up which places offer special viewings this fall and grab spots.

Things to see · in no particular order

  • Gion

    The famous historic district is wonderful for photos and shopping. Nearby Yasaka Pagoda looks unreal at sunrise and sunset.

  • Kiyomizu-dera Temple

    Early

    A hillside temple with a huge wooden stage and great surrounding streets. It gets especially busy, so go early.

  • Fushimi Inari

    Early

    Thousands of red gates and an optional full hike to the top; you do not have to finish it. Go early.

  • Kinkaku-ji

    The Golden Pavilion, surrounded by a beautiful park.

  • Arashiyama

    Early

    Spend a day 30–45 minutes from the center with the bamboo forest, river and bridge, and monkey park. Get an early start.

  • Sagano Romantic Sightseeing Train

    Book ahead

    A scenic Arashiyama train that runs most of the year but not winter, especially gorgeous in fall. Book when tickets release and try for open-air car 5, “The Rich”—no glass, and naturally everyone wants it.

  • Rurikoin

    Book ahead

    Polished tables beside corner windows reflect the garden like an infinity pool. Yes, it became an Instagram thing; it is still worth it. Peak fall requires advance tickets.

  • Uji

    Optional

    A scenic town south of Kyoto, supposedly matcha’s birthplace. Byodo-in is gorgeous and appears on the ¥10 coin, but this is probably not a first-visit priority unless something specific draws you.

Where to stay

Park Hyatt Kyoto

We have stayed at many nice hotels, and this one is special enough to qualify as an attraction. It is very pricey, but worth a look if you feel like springing for it. Points people can transfer Chase or Bilt rewards to Hyatt.

Where to eat

  • Mouriya Gion

    Book ahead

    Kobe beef; reservation required.

  • Kaiten Sushi Ginza Onodera Kyoto

    The same conveyor-belt standout recommended in Tokyo.

  • Sen no Kaze

    Walk-in

    Ramen; walk-in.

  • Hikiniku to Come Kyoto

    The same must-go hamburg steak as in Tokyo.

  • Yusaitei

    Book ahead

    An izakaya-style assortment of great dishes in a fun atmosphere; reservation required.

City 03

Osaka

Think smaller-scale Tokyo, famous for food and personality. There is not a ton of traditional sightseeing, so one or two days is typical—but it is fantastic for eating your way through and catching up on shopping.

Things to do · in no particular order

  • Dotonbori

    The famous neon canal district with the Glico running man sign from all the photos. Endless food. There’s also a huge Don Quijote here if you haven’t experienced one yet.

  • Tsutenkaku & around

    Similar retro-neon energy, different sights, and lots more food.

  • Umeda Sky Building

    The best city view in Osaka. Tickets are needed but easy to get.

  • A food tour

    Osaka is the right city for sampling many dishes and meeting other travelers.

  • teamLab Botanical Garden

    Optional

    The outdoor cousin of Tokyo’s exhibits. Pick Tokyo if choosing one; worthwhile if you love them.

  • Universal Studios Japan

    Book ahead

    We have not visited, but it is famous for Super Nintendo World if you are into that. Tickets in advance.

Where to eat

Yakitori Torisen Book ahead

Amazing yakitori with a great chef; a reservation is required. This is the place we would go back to first.

Day trips & farther afield

More Japan

  • Hakone

    Possible as a Tokyo day trip, but we stayed one night and recommend it. This mountain area has onsen, Fuji views, lovely ryokan, and the “Hakone loop”: cable car, ropeway, and lake crossing on a pirate ship—yes, a pirate ship. November combines foliage with clear-air Fuji views.

  • Nara

    An easy Kyoto or Osaka day trip. Free-roaming deer literally bow when fed; animal lovers will delight, and the park also has beautiful temples.

  • Kobe

    Optional

    We day-tripped from Osaka specifically to eat Kobe beef in Kobe and valued the story. Real Kobe beef exists in every major city, so skip this unless the idea means something to you.

  • Hiroshima & Miyajima

    Hiroshima has a deeply moving museum dedicated to the WWII atomic bombing. Nearby Miyajima has beautiful temples and the famous “floating” torii gate.

  • Hokkaido

    Japan’s northern island offers mountains, incredible food, world-class winter skiing, and summer flower fields—a good destination almost any time of year.

  • Snow monkeys (Nagano)

    A winter-specific Tokyo day trip to wild monkeys lounging in natural hot springs. Experience warns us: the approach is a 45-minute hike on snowy, slippery trails, so bring real footwear. Highly prefer an organized Tokyo bus tour over the train-and-bus combination.

If you come back

Japan by season

  • Spring · March/April

    Cherry blossoms make this the most popular season, but many travelers cross the world and miss them: bloom windows are short, regional, hard to predict, and worsening with climate change. Arrive near the estimate and stay flexible enough to chase the bloom. Plum blossoms can start in mid-February and be just as beautiful with a fraction of the crowds.

  • Winter · mid-December through February

    Underrated and blissfully uncrowded. Main tourist areas remain fairly mild (40s–50s) and dry. There are no blossoms or foliage, but nearly everything else remains possible, plus snow monkeys, world-class Hokkaido skiing, and peak strawberry season.

  • Summer

    Every single person we have asked says the same thing: don’t. It is extremely hot and humid, with downpours that can wreck outdoor plans—and it intensifies every year.
